Oral bioavailability (F) is the fraction of an orally administered drug that reaches systemic circulation in unchanged form. It is determined by three sequential barriers: absorption from the gastrointestinal lumen across the intestinal epithelium, first-pass metabolism in the gut wall (primarily by CYP3A4), and first-pass metabolism in the liver (by CYP enzymes and conjugation). This dataset uses a binary threshold of F ≥ 20% vs F < 20%.

Molecular features favoring high oral bioavailability: compliance with Lipinski's Rule of Five (MW ≤ 500, logP ≤ 5, HBD ≤ 5, HBA ≤ 10) — these are necessary but not sufficient conditions for oral absorption. Moderate lipophilicity (logP 1-3) balances membrane permeability with aqueous solubility. Low polar surface area (TPSA < 140 Å²) facilitates transcellular passive diffusion. Limited molecular flexibility (rotatable bonds ≤ 10) reduces the entropic penalty for adopting a membrane-permeable conformation. Adequate aqueous solubility ensures dissolution in GI fluids. Metabolic stability — resistance to CYP-mediated oxidation and Phase II conjugation — preserves the parent compound through first-pass metabolism.

Features associated with poor bioavailability: high MW (>500 Da) and excessive hydrogen bonding capacity reduce membrane permeability. Very high lipophilicity (logP > 5) causes poor aqueous solubility despite good permeability. High TPSA (>140 Å²) strongly correlates with poor absorption. Many rotatable bonds (>10) reduce the probability of adopting a compact, membrane-permeable conformation. Susceptibility to efflux by P-glycoprotein (Pgp) at the intestinal epithelium reduces net absorption. Labile metabolic soft spots — unhindered aromatic rings, benzylic positions, N-dealkylation sites — lead to rapid first-pass metabolism. Compounds that are substrates of CYP3A4 (the dominant gut-wall and hepatic CYP) face extensive first-pass extraction.
