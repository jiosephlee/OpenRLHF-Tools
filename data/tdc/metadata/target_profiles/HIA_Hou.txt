Human intestinal absorption (HIA) measures the fraction of an orally administered drug absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract into the portal circulation. This is the first barrier to oral bioavailability — a drug must cross the intestinal epithelium before it encounters hepatic first-pass metabolism. Absorption occurs primarily via passive transcellular diffusion through the lipid bilayer of enterocytes, though carrier-mediated uptake (e.g., PEPT1 for peptide-like drugs, OATP for anionic compounds) and paracellular transport (for very small, hydrophilic molecules) also contribute.

Molecular features favoring high intestinal absorption: moderate lipophilicity (logP 1-4) enables passive diffusion across the enterocyte membrane. Low polar surface area (TPSA < 120 Å²) is one of the strongest predictors — the desolvation cost of burying polar groups in the lipid bilayer is the primary barrier to transcellular transport. Limited hydrogen bond donors (HBD ≤ 3-4) are more important than acceptors because donors form stronger interactions with water that must be broken during membrane partitioning. Molecular weight below 500 Da and moderate flexibility (rotatable bonds ≤ 10) favor absorption. Adequate aqueous solubility in GI fluids (pH 1-7.4) ensures the drug is dissolved and available for absorption.

Features associated with poor absorption: high TPSA (>140 Å²) is the strongest negative predictor. Zwitterionic or multiply charged species at intestinal pH have poor membrane permeability. Very polar, highly hydrophilic molecules (logP < -1) cannot partition into the lipid bilayer. Large, rigid molecules with MW > 600 Da have difficulty crossing the membrane. Compounds subject to active efflux by P-glycoprotein (Pgp/ABCB1) or BCRP (ABCG2) at the apical enterocyte membrane experience reduced net absorption — Pgp substrates are pumped back into the lumen after partial absorption. Acid-labile compounds may degrade in gastric pH before reaching the absorptive surface of the small intestine.
