The Ames test is a bacterial reverse mutation assay that detects compounds capable of inducing genetic damage. Histidine-dependent Salmonella typhimurium (and sometimes E. coli tryptophan auxotrophs) are exposed to the test compound; mutagenic compounds cause reversion mutations that restore the bacteria's ability to synthesize the required amino acid, producing visible colonies. The test is run both with and without metabolic activation (rat liver S9 fraction) to detect pro-mutagens that require CYP-mediated bioactivation.

Mutagenicity in the Ames test arises through several distinct mechanisms. Direct-acting mutagens are electrophilic species that react covalently with DNA bases — alkylating agents (epoxides, aziridines, nitrogen mustards, alkyl halides), Michael acceptors (α,β-unsaturated carbonyls, acrylates, vinyl sulfones), and acylating agents. Pro-mutagens require metabolic activation: aromatic amines are N-hydroxylated by CYP1A2 to form reactive hydroxylamines/nitrenium ions that form DNA adducts; polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are epoxidized by CYP1A1/1B1 to form diol-epoxides; nitroaromatics are reduced to reactive nitroso and hydroxylamine intermediates. DNA intercalators — large planar polycyclic molecules — can cause frameshift mutations by inserting between base pairs and disrupting replication fidelity.

Structural features associated with mutagenicity: aromatic amines and their acetylated derivatives (especially primary aromatic amines —NH₂ on an aromatic ring), nitroaromatic groups (—NO₂ on aromatic rings — reduced to reactive species), alkylating functional groups (epoxides, aziridines, alkyl halides, nitrogen mustards), Michael acceptors (α,β-unsaturated carbonyls adjacent to electron-withdrawing groups), aromatic nitroso groups, hydrazines and hydrazides, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons with bay regions (facilitating diol-epoxide formation), and N-nitroso compounds. Features arguing against mutagenicity: absence of electrophilic or DNA-reactive functional groups, absence of aromatic amines or nitro groups, steric shielding of potentially reactive sites, high molecular weight and steric bulk that prevents DNA intercalation or minor groove binding, and the presence of rapid detoxification handles (e.g., carboxylic acids that promote glucuronidation and clearance before DNA damage occurs).
