The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is formed by brain capillary endothelial cells connected by tight junctions, surrounded by pericytes and astrocyte end-feet. Unlike peripheral capillaries, the BBB has no fenestrations and minimal pinocytosis, so drug entry into the CNS requires either passive transcellular diffusion through the endothelial cell membranes or carrier-mediated transport. Efflux transporters — particularly P-glycoprotein (Pgp/ABCB1) and BCRP (ABCG2) — on the luminal side actively pump many compounds back into the blood, creating an additional barrier beyond simple membrane permeability.

Molecular features favoring BBB penetration: moderate lipophilicity (logP 1-3) provides the driving force for passive diffusion across the endothelial lipid bilayer. Low polar surface area (TPSA < 70-80 Å²) is the strongest single predictor of BBB penetration — the tight junctions eliminate the paracellular route, so transcellular diffusion is the only passive pathway. Few hydrogen bond donors (HBD ≤ 1-2) — more restrictive than for intestinal absorption because the BBB endothelium is less permeable. Small to moderate molecular weight (MW < 450 Da). Low overall hydrogen bonding capacity. Most successful CNS drugs are small, moderately lipophilic, and have minimal polar surface area. Being a substrate of nutrient transporters (LAT1 for amino acid-like drugs, GLUT1 for glucose-like structures) can facilitate active uptake.

Features associated with poor BBB penetration: high TPSA (> 90 Å²) strongly predicts exclusion from the CNS. Multiple hydrogen bond donors (HBD ≥ 3) — each additional donor roughly halves BBB permeability. Compounds that are P-glycoprotein substrates are actively effluxed from the brain — basic amines with MW > 400 and moderate lipophilicity are common Pgp substrates. Charged molecules at pH 7.4 (carboxylates, sulfonates, phosphates, quaternary amines) have very poor passive BBB penetration. High molecular weight (> 500 Da). Very high lipophilicity (logP > 5) can paradoxically reduce BBB penetration due to excessive plasma protein binding and poor aqueous solubility, which reduce the free fraction available for diffusion.
