Context: The Parallel Artificial Membrane Permeability Assay (PAMPA) measures passive transcellular permeability by quantifying how much compound diffuses across an artificial lipid membrane (typically a phospholipid mixture on a filter support) from a donor to an acceptor compartment. Unlike Caco-2 cell assays, PAMPA has no active transporters, no efflux pumps, and no paracellular pathway — it isolates pure passive diffusion. The NCATS variant uses a standardized protocol across a large compound library.

The rate of passive diffusion is governed by the drug's ability to partition from aqueous solution into the lipid bilayer, diffuse across it, and re-partition into the aqueous phase on the other side. This is primarily determined by the molecule's desolvation cost — how much energy is required to strip away the hydration shell of polar groups before entering the hydrophobic membrane interior.

Molecular features favoring high PAMPA permeability: low polar surface area (TPSA < 80 Å² for high permeability, 80-120 Å² for moderate) is the single most predictive descriptor. Moderate lipophilicity (logP 1-4) provides the driving force for membrane partitioning. Molecular weight < 500 Da. Intramolecular hydrogen bonds can "mask" polar groups and improve permeability beyond what TPSA alone would predict — this is exploited in cyclic peptide drug design. Compact, low-flexibility conformations reduce the entropic cost of membrane insertion.

Features associated with poor PAMPA permeability: high TPSA (>120 Å²) is the dominant negative predictor. Multiple hydrogen bond donors (HBD ≥ 4) strongly reduce permeability. Charged species at the assay pH (typically pH 7.4) — carboxylates, phosphates, quaternary amines — have very poor passive diffusion because the charge cannot be buried in the lipid interior.
