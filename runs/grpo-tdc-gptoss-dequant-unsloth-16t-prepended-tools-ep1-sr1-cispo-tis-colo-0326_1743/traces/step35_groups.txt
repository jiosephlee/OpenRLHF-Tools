The rate of passive diffusion is governed by the drug's ability to partition from aqueous solution into the lipid bilayer, diffuse across it, and re-partition into the aqueous phase on the other side. This is primarily determined by the molecule's desolvation cost — how much energy is required to strip away the hydration shell of polar groups before entering the hydrophobic membrane interior.

Molecular features favoring high PAMPA permeability: low polar surface area (TPSA < 80 Å² for high permeability, 80-120 Å² for moderate) is the single most predictive descriptor. Intramolecular hydrogen bonds can "mask" polar groups and improve permeability beyond what TPSA alone would predict — this is exploited in cyclic peptide drug design. Compact, low-flexibility conformations reduce the entropic cost of membrane insertion.

Features associated with poor PAMPA permeability: high TPSA (>120 Å²) is the dominant negative predictor. Multiple hydrogen bond donors (HBD ≥ 4) strongly reduce permeability.
