Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) is the leading cause of acute liver failure in the developed world and the most common reason for post-market drug withdrawal. The liver is uniquely vulnerable because it is the primary site of drug metabolism — hepatocytes express high levels of CYP enzymes and conjugation machinery, and the portal circulation delivers orally absorbed drugs at high concentrations directly to the liver before systemic distribution.

DILI arises through several mechanisms. Intrinsic (dose-dependent) hepatotoxicity occurs when a drug or its metabolite is directly cytotoxic to hepatocytes — acetaminophen (via NAPQI, its reactive quinone imine metabolite formed by CYP2E1) is the classic example. Idiosyncratic DILI is dose-independent and unpredictable, often involving immune-mediated mechanisms: reactive metabolites form covalent adducts with liver proteins, which can trigger adaptive immune responses (hapten hypothesis) or disrupt immune tolerance. Mitochondrial toxicity — inhibition of electron transport chain complexes or mitochondrial DNA polymerase gamma — is another important mechanism. Bile salt export pump (BSEP/ABCB11) inhibition causes cholestatic liver injury by preventing bile acid secretion.

Structural features associated with DILI risk: aniline and para-aminophenol motifs (bioactivated to reactive quinone imines via CYP oxidation and further to protein-reactive nitroso intermediates). Nitroaromatic groups (reduced to reactive hydroxylamine/nitroso species). Thiophene rings (S-oxidized to reactive epoxides or sulfoxides). Furan rings (oxidized to reactive cis-enedial intermediates). Hydrazine and acylhydrazide groups (forming reactive acyl radicals). Carboxylic acids — while common in safe drugs, they form acyl glucuronides that can covalently modify proteins. Michael acceptors and other electrophilic centers. High lipophilicity (logP > 3) with extensive hepatic metabolism increases liver exposure to reactive intermediates. High daily dose (>100 mg/day) is a strong epidemiological risk factor.

Features associated with lower DILI risk: metabolically stable scaffolds without bioactivation-prone functional groups. Low daily dose. Low lipophilicity (reduced hepatic extraction). Absence of known reactive metabolite precursors. Compounds primarily eliminated renally rather than hepatically.
