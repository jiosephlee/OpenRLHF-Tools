Skin sensitization (allergic contact dermatitis) is an immune-mediated adverse reaction triggered by repeated dermal exposure to a chemical. It proceeds through two phases: induction (the chemical penetrates the skin, reacts covalently with skin proteins to form hapten-protein conjugates, which are processed by dendritic cells and presented to T cells, priming the adaptive immune system) and elicitation (subsequent exposure triggers a T cell-mediated inflammatory response). The key molecular event is haptenation — covalent modification of skin proteins by the chemical or its metabolite.

For a chemical to act as a skin sensitizer, it must: (1) penetrate the stratum corneum (the skin's outer barrier), (2) be electrophilic or become electrophilic after abiotic or enzymatic activation in the skin, and (3) react covalently with nucleophilic amino acid residues (primarily cysteine thiol and lysine amine) on skin proteins. The reaction mechanism determines the sensitization potency — strong electrophiles that react with both cysteine and lysine (e.g., acid anhydrides, isocyanates) tend to be stronger sensitizers than those reacting with only one nucleophile.

Structural features associated with skin sensitization: Michael acceptors (α,β-unsaturated carbonyls, acrylates, methacrylates — react with cysteine via 1,4-addition). Acylating agents (acid anhydrides, acid chlorides, isocyanates, isothiocyanates — react with lysine amines). SN2 electrophiles (alkyl halides, epoxides, activated esters, sulfonates — react with cysteine). SNAr electrophiles (electron-poor aromatic rings with leaving groups — react with cysteine). Schiff base formers (aldehydes — react with lysine to form imines). Pro-haptens requiring metabolic activation: aromatic amines (oxidized to nitroso/hydroxylamine intermediates), phenols and catechols (oxidized to quinones), and thiols (oxidized to disulfides or sulfenic acids). Moderate lipophilicity (logP 1-4) favors skin penetration.

Features arguing against skin sensitization: absence of electrophilic functional groups or their metabolic precursors. Fully saturated, non-reactive molecules (alkanes, ethers, simple alcohols). Very hydrophilic molecules (logP < -1) that cannot penetrate the stratum corneum. Very large molecules (MW > 500) that are too bulky for skin penetration. Steric shielding of potentially reactive centers. Carboxylic acids — the negative charge at physiological pH disfavors both skin penetration and nucleophilic substitution reactions.
