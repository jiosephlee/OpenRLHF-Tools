Carcinogenicity is the ability of a substance to cause cancer through sustained genetic damage, epigenetic changes, or chronic disruption of cellular growth control. This dataset (Lagunin) classifies compounds as carcinogens or non-carcinogens based on long-term rodent bioassay data (typically 2-year studies in rats and mice), which is the regulatory gold standard for carcinogenicity assessment.

Carcinogenicity can arise through genotoxic or non-genotoxic mechanisms. Genotoxic carcinogens directly damage DNA — this overlaps substantially with Ames mutagenicity but also includes clastogens (chromosome breakers) and aneugens (spindle poisons) that are not detected by the Ames test. Non-genotoxic carcinogens promote cancer through sustained receptor activation (e.g., PPARα agonists causing hepatocarcinogenesis in rodents), chronic cytotoxicity with compensatory proliferation, hormonal disruption (estrogenic or androgenic compounds driving hormone-dependent tumors), immunosuppression, or epigenetic changes. Non-genotoxic carcinogenesis is harder to predict from structure because it depends on the specific biological pathway perturbed.

Structural features associated with carcinogenicity: all the genotoxic alerts from the Ames test apply — aromatic amines, nitroaromatics, alkylating agents (epoxides, aziridines, nitrogen mustards), polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons with bay regions, N-nitroso compounds, hydrazines. Additionally: compounds that undergo metabolic activation to reactive intermediates (aflatoxin-like structures, furan rings that form cis-enedial metabolites), peroxisome proliferators (fibrate-like structures — though this mechanism may be rodent-specific), estrogenic scaffolds (stilbenes, bisphenol-like structures), and immunosuppressive structures.

Features arguing against carcinogenicity: absence of DNA-reactive functional groups and their metabolic precursors. Low reactivity — metabolically stable scaffolds without electrophilic centers. Small, polar molecules that are rapidly cleared without bioaccumulation. Compounds without known receptor-mediated proliferative effects. The presence of antioxidant functional groups (phenolic OH in certain contexts) can be protective, though phenols can also be bioactivated to quinones.
